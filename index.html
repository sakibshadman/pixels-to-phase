Pixels-to-Phase

Reliability-Aware Latent State-Space Modeling for Contactless Respiratory Estimation

Shadman Sakib1, Co-author Name1, Co-author Name1

1University of Maryland, Baltimore County

Papersoon Supplementarysoon arXivsoon Codesoon Datasetsoon Video BibTeX

Pixels-to-Phase treats breathing as a hidden phase on the unit circle. Each frame, it predicts the phase from learned dynamics, then corrects it with the video only as much as the video can be trusted.

Abstract

Contactless respiratory estimation from video is challenging because respiratory motion is weak, spatially localized, and easily corrupted by nuisance motion and poor visual conditions. We introduce Pixels-to-Phase, a reliability-aware latent state-space model that represents respiration as a partially observed phase process and separates temporal state propagation from observation-dependent correction. The method localizes persistent respiratory support, constructs visual phase observations from complementary mechanical and semantic cues, and uses estimated observation reliability to control how strongly each visual observation updates the latent respiratory state. Persistent motion evidence and semantic observability therefore contribute to measurement construction, while temporal consistency and motion-quality cues determine when that measurement should be trusted. This formulation preserves short-term respiratory continuity when visual evidence is unreliable while allowing the state to re-anchor when informative observations return. Across RGB, NIR, and thermal benchmarks, Pixels-to-Phase reduces average respiratory-rate MAE from 1.69 BPM for the strongest baseline to 1.51 BPM. Held-out-dataset evaluation further demonstrates consistent improvements across changes in sensing modality, subject population, and acquisition conditions. Targeted analyses show that reliability-aware correction is particularly beneficial under degraded visual evidence while the latent state remains responsive to genuine respiratory transitions.

Why reliability matters

Breathing shows up in video as a few pixels of chest, shoulder and clothing motion. Camera shake, a moving hand or a dark room can swamp it for seconds at a time. A model that maps each frame straight to a waveform has no way to tell an unreliable frame from a real change in breathing, so it follows the noise.

Method

Respiration is a partially observed phase process. The state lives on the unit circle. The model propagates it with learned dynamics, builds a visual observation of it from motion and semantic cues, and lets an estimated reliability decide how much that observation counts. Four parts, one causal loop

Support localization decides where to look. Visual evidence construction turns that region into a phase observation . Phase dynamics rotate the previous state into a prior . A reliability head scores the current observation, and the update blends prior and observation by that score. Every update is causal, and the full pipeline runs at about 207 frames per second on one NVIDIA H200; the recurrent update itself takes about 1.8 ms per frame.

1. Support localization

Dense RAFT-Large flow on ten sampled frame pairs proposes anchors, and long-range point tracking follows them. Each trajectory is kept only if it is persistent, forward–backward consistent and concentrated in the 0.10–0.70 Hz respiratory band. The kept tracks and a dense temporal-consensus mask form the support prior . It does not estimate respiration; it tells later stages where persistent, breathing-like motion is.

2. Visual evidence construction

Inside the support, PhaseFlow projects coarse-to-fine Lucas–Kanade displacement onto the image gradient, giving signed mechanical evidence aligned with image structure. A two-branch motion encoder adds learned spatiotemporal features. A frozen CLIP ViT-B/16 scores surface visibility, occlusion, interfering content and acquisition quality. Support-guided pooling combines them into the phase observation .

3. Phase dynamics

A GRU over past states predicts the breathing rate , constrained to the respiratory range, and the previous corrected state is rotated forward by it. This prior keeps the rhythm going when the current video says little; with no new observations, waveform correlation stays at 0.92 one second ahead and 0.85 three seconds ahead.

4. Reliability-aware update

The reliability head reads semantic observability, forward–backward flow inconsistency, RAFT versus Lucas–Kanade disagreement, respiratory-band concentration and prior–observation disagreement, and outputs . High re-anchors the state to the video; low keeps the propagated prior. Waveform, phase, rate and reliability are read from the corrected state, and the rate uses only frames above the update threshold .

Results

We evaluate on five respiratory video benchmarks spanning RGB, NIR and thermal sensing: AIR-400, MPSC-RR, Sleep, OVRM and our In-House Dataset v1.0. Pixels-to-Phase has the lowest average respiratory-rate error, and the gain over FLOW, the strongest baseline, holds when the test dataset is never seen in training.

When the gate matters

The benefit of reliability-aware correction is small on clean video and grows under weak motion, occlusion, motion artifacts and low-light NIR. Lower estimated reliability tracks larger error: RR error rises from 1.08 BPM in high-reliability intervals to 1.57 and 2.46 BPM at intermediate and low reliability.

In-House Dataset v1.0

Release in preparation

A versioned RGB corpus collected under an IRB-approved protocol with informed consent. It varies the things that make breathing hard to see: posture, viewpoint, subject–camera distance, illumination, clothing texture and body motion. It is used in the paper as an additional evaluation resource next to the public benchmarks.

Recordings
73 RGB videos: 50 indoor and 23 outdoor
Indoor
1920 × 1080 at 30 fps, about 2–3.5 min each, with a synchronized respiratory-belt reference. Spontaneous and paced breathing (slow, regular, fast); frontal seated, top-angular, backward-view and lying/supine views.
Outdoor
One-minute recordings at 4, 5, 6, 7, 9, 11, 13 and 15 m. Each follows about 20 s of normal breathing, 20 s of breath holding and 20 s of forced breathing.
Protocol
5-fold subject-wise cross-validation; recordings from one subject never cross partitions.
Access
Because the videos can identify people, de-identified recordings are shared on reasonable request, subject to IRB, institutional and data-sharing requirements. This is a respiratory-monitoring research dataset, not a clinical diagnostic or apnea dataset.
